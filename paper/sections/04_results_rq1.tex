\section{RQ1: Gamification}
\label{sec:rq1}

The self-tracking literature says gamified mechanics --- streaks, scores,
badges --- create guilt and anxiety in users who fall behind. Prayer trackers
add exactly these mechanics to the five daily prayers, so RQ1 asks whether this
happens here. It does not, at least not in the form the literature predicts.
Gamifying a prayer tracker does not move sentiment either way, and guilt
language is actually rarer in tracker discourse than elsewhere in the corpus.
What users resent instead is a record they cannot trust and a prompt that
never arrives.

\subsection{Scope}

We define ``tracker discourse'' as any review carrying one of three aspect
tags: \texttt{prayer\_tracker}, \texttt{tracker\_score}, or
\texttt{goal\_system} --- 6,285 reviews across 25 of the 26 apps.
\texttt{prayer\_tracker} (78\% precision) accounts for 5,907 of these; the
other two tags, both far noisier (0\% precision against their gold rows),
contribute the remaining 378 (6.0\%). Noise at this level weakens a
distributional signal rather than creating one. The coding passes below draw
their own separate samples and do not depend on this boundary.

\subsection{No Effect from the Tracker Mechanic, but a Bimodal Split}

Comparing sentiment across three tiers --- no tracker, a plain tracker, a
tracker with a numeric score --- the primary mixed-effects model failed to
converge, so we report a cluster-robust sensitivity fit instead. Neither tier
moves sentiment meaningfully: a plain tracker gives \(\beta=-0.087\) (95\% CI
\([-0.332, 0.158]\)) and a scored tracker \(\beta=-0.134\)
(\([-0.415, 0.147]\)), both intervals comfortably spanning zero.

But a mean was never the right test. The literature's love/hate prediction is
about the \emph{shape} of the sentiment distribution, not its centre, and
Hartigan's dip test confirms exactly that shape: \(D=0.118\), \(p<10^{-16}\)
(Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-bimodal}). Tracker sentiment splits toward the two poles,
users loving the tracker or resenting it, with little middle ground --- which
is why the qualitative coding below, not the tier comparison, carries the real
answer to RQ1.

\subsection{Guilt Is Rare, Not Common}

If streak anxiety were driving the negative half of that split, guilt language
should appear more often in tracker discourse than elsewhere. It does not.
Matching guilt/anxiety keywords against motivation keywords, the ratio inside
tracker reviews is 0.26, against 0.86 elsewhere in the corpus (Fisher exact
OR \(=0.283\), \(p=3.5\times10^{-11}\)) (Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-guilt}). These
lists were never gold-validated, so we treat this as a fact about word
frequency, not a validated guilt measurement --- the real evidence is the hand
coding below, which points the same direction. Where guilt-adjacent language
does appear, it is often light-hearted, folded into praise:

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``I love the app. My everyday friend who reminds me to pray, and makes
sure that I feel guilty if I don't [\normalfont sweat-smile emoji\itshape] I
recommend to everyone who wants to start praying, or keep the habit in a
consistent manner!''
\end{quote}
\hspace*{1em}--- Athan: Prayer Times \& Al Quran, 5 stars

\subsection{Two Independent Coding Passes}
\label{sec:rq1-coding}

We hand-coded two samples of reviews expressing tracker dissatisfaction. The
first pass (50 reviews) came entirely from one app, Athan, an artefact of a
selection-rule tie-break rather than evidence that one app dominates
complaints. The second pass (30 reviews) corrected this, sampling across the
other 22 apps. Pass 1 yielded 33 open codes (\(n=49\) after exclusions); pass 2
yielded 25 codes (\(n=28\)) plus two new themes. We never pool the two passes,
since that would silently re-weight the ecosystem toward Athan
(Table~\ref{tab:rq1-themes}).

Guilt or judgment itself is rare in both passes: 2 of 49 coded complaints in
pass 1 (4.1\%), 1 of 28 in pass 2 (3.6\%). A hypothesis this prominent in the
self-tracking literature showing up at 4\% or below, in two independently drawn
samples, looks like a real null result, not an underpowered one.

The two passes also show a broader hazard for review-mining studies: themes A,
B, and F (redesign, retroactive logging, monetisation versus religious
purpose) together make up 51\% of the single-app pass's complaints but only
4\% of the cross-app pass's --- single-app sampling manufacturing what looks
like a genre-wide theme.

\subsection{What Dissatisfaction Is Actually About}

Three themes replicate across both samples, in the same rank order.

\textbf{Prompting failure dominates, at 46\% of the cross-app pass.} The
tracker does not fail because logging is tedious --- it fails because the app
never reliably prompts: location detection breaks, adhan playback does not
fire, notifications get lost, calendars drift out of sync.

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``the adhan is not working unless I open the app without logging out
of it till prayer time''
\end{quote}
\hspace*{1em}--- Pray Watch, 1 star

\textbf{Record trust replicates almost exactly}, at 22\% in pass 1 and 21\% in
pass 2: users lose logged history, watch counts regress, or the tracker
silently disables itself --- often inside reviews that otherwise praise the
app.

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``I would give it a 5 star, but the prayers that I log on the
previous days or current day would sometimes disappear. Please fix this!''
\end{quote}
\hspace*{1em}--- Athan: Prayer Times \& Al Quran, 4 stars

\textbf{Normative mismatch is the smallest replicating theme}, at roughly 11\%
in both passes, and it is not guilt. Where a review raises a genuine normative
complaint, it is that the app \emph{models worship incorrectly} --- no
menstrual exemption, no supererogatory (\emph{sunnah}) prayer support, imposed
goals that override the user's own targets:

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``It's a really good app but I'm taking 1 star away cause I wish
there was some option for menstruating women cuz my overall score on prayer
tracker becomes really low when I can't pray for my period.''
\end{quote}
\hspace*{1em}--- Deen - Islamic App, 3 stars

Menstrual-exemption complaints turn up independently in both passes, from
different apps --- direct evidence we return to in RQ4 (\S\ref{sec:rq4}).

\subsection{One Application's Episode}

Two of the six original themes, redesign-as-regression (20\% of pass 1) and
retroactive-logging-denied (10\%), disappear entirely in the cross-app pass.
Both trace to a single Athan redesign that broke an existing logging workflow
and removed retroactive logging. We report this as an application-specific
episode, not an ecosystem theme --- folding it in would overstate how much
tracker dissatisfaction is really about record integrity in general. Overall,
tracker dissatisfaction is about the app failing to prompt reliably and failing
to keep a trustworthy record, and where a normative objection surfaces, it
concerns the app's model of worship rather than pressure to perform.
