\section{RQ2: Accuracy Versus Interface}
\label{sec:rq2}

A second common framing in the review-mining and mobile-HCI literature treats
accuracy and interface polish as a trade-off users weigh against one another.
RQ2 asks whether devotional-app users punish inaccuracy more harshly than a
poor interface. As a class, they do not --- but that is the wrong comparison:
the class average hides a severity gradient inside accuracy itself, and the
rating signal developers optimise against conceals the worst of it.

\subsection{As Classes, Accuracy and Interface Cost the Same}

Using M1, a review-level mixed-effects model of star rating against complaint
aspect (\(n = 324{,}687\); all coefficients in this section survive
Benjamini--Hochberg correction at \(q < 0.05\)), the accuracy family ---
prayer-time accuracy, qibla, madhab, calculation method --- averages
\(-0.647\) stars against \(-0.600\) for \texttt{ui\_design}, a gap under a
twentieth of a star. The intuition that users punish a wrong qibla or prayer
time more harshly than a cluttered screen is not supported at the level of the
complaint class.

\subsection{The Class Average Conceals a Severity Gradient}

Table~\ref{tab:rq2-coefficients} and Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-coefficients} give the
spread underlying that average. Accuracy is not one thing: prayer-time accuracy
alone costs \(-1.112\) stars (95\% CI \([-1.142, -1.083]\)), 1.85 times the
interface penalty, with an interval disjoint from \texttt{ui\_design}'s. The
family mean is pulled down by \texttt{madhab} (\(-0.324\)) and \texttt{qibla}
(\(-0.550\)), both rarer and milder complaints. Getting the prayer time wrong
is among the most costly failures in the corpus; getting the madhab option
wrong is among the least --- and \texttt{madhab} reverses sign in an ordinal
robustness check (\(\beta = +0.216\), \(p = 0.649\)), so no directional claim
rests on it either way.

\subsection{Qibla Failure Is Instability, Not Miscalculation}

The larger of the two qibla-related topics (1,771 reviews) is labelled around
wrong direction, outweighing a smaller one (698) labelled around an inaccurate
compass pointer. In the larger topic's representative reviews the complaint is
not that the app computes the wrong bearing: users describe the compass
returning a different bearing from the same physical spot as the phone is
rotated. This is a sensor and calibration-handling problem rather than an error
in the qibla computation, and the two call for different fixes.

\subsection{Star Ratings Hide Accuracy Defects}

The strongest result in RQ2 is methodological. Of 7,942 reviews carrying at
least one negative accuracy-family complaint, 3,306 (41.6\%) awarded four or
five stars and 1,830 (23.0\%) the maximum; the distribution runs 2,319
one-star, 914 two-star, 1,403 three-star, 1,476 four-star, 1,830 five-star.
Users report that the prayer time or qibla direction is wrong and rate the
application highly in the same review. Any prioritisation process reading
defect severity off the star distribution --- the signal most developers and
ranking systems optimise against --- will under-weight accuracy defects by a
wide margin, because a substantial share of the reviews naming them never
produce a low rating.

\subsection{Advertising Costs More Than Any Accuracy Complaint}

One coefficient falls outside the accuracy/interface contrast entirely:
intrusive advertising costs \(-1.439\) stars, above every accuracy-family term
including prayer-time accuracy. We return to advertising as a recurring cost in
\S\ref{sec:rq5} and \S\ref{sec:rq6}.
