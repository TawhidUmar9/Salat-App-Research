\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Corpus and Data Collection}
\label{sec:method-corpus}

We compiled a corpus of reviews for 26 Islamic prayer-time and devotional
applications on the Google Play Store, collected on 2026-08-19 and spanning
each application's review history available at that date. Reviews and app-level
metadata (store description, install count, rating) were scraped, yielding
732,194 reviews (Figure~\ref{fig:corpus}).

Applications were selected purposively. We prioritised high install and review
counts, so per-app volume would support app-level comparison, and preferred
applications whose primary purpose is salat --- prayer times, adhan, qibla,
tracking --- over general Islamic-lifestyle or educational ones, keeping a few
lower-volume ones where they were the only instance of a functional category. The set is neither a census nor a random sample and no target size
was fixed in advance; the ecosystem-level claims here are claims about these 26
applications, which we take to cover the high-traffic core of the category
rather than its long tail.

Reviews were requested with the store locale set to English and the United
States (\texttt{lang=en}, \texttt{country=us}), sorted newest-first. This does
not yield a purely English corpus --- 48,265 of the 324,687 texted reviews were
later identified as non-English (\S\ref{sec:method-language}) --- but it does
mean the corpus is the English/US-locale slice of each application's reviews
rather than its global review population. Every application was collected under
identical parameters, so cross-application comparisons remain like-for-like;
what the decision constrains is generalisation (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Language Filtering and Analysis Sub-corpora}
\label{sec:method-language}

Reviews carrying review text form an analysed sub-corpus of 324,687
(\(C_{\mathrm{text}}\)). Language identification with fastText gives the
English subset used for topic modelling, 276,422 reviews
(\(C_{\mathrm{en}}\)).

\subsection{Sentiment Classification}
\label{sec:method-sentiment}

Reviews were scored with VADER and with a RoBERTa sentiment classifier. The
RoBERTa label is reported throughout; VADER is retained only as a comparison
point. Across all 324,687 texted reviews
(Figure~\ref{fig:sentiment-agreement}), RoBERTa agrees with the star-derived
label at Cohen's \(\kappa = 0.466\) against 0.210 for VADER, the two
classifiers agreeing at 0.385. Star ratings are not ground truth --- RQ2 is in
part an argument that they are not --- but RoBERTa tracking them more than
twice as closely is why we report it.

We also hand-labelled a 500-document gold set into four categories (Positive,
Neutral, Negative, Mixed); on the 100 rows coded by both annotators, raw
agreement was 89.0\% and Krippendorff's \(\alpha = 0.824\). This is an
inter-annotator reliability figure, not a measurement of classifier accuracy,
which we did not compute. Sentiment therefore enters as a comparatively, rather
than absolutely, validated variable, and the claims resting most heavily on it
--- tracker-sentiment bimodality (\S\ref{sec:rq1}) and the feature-level
negative rates behind RQ6a --- should be read with that gap in mind.

\subsection{Aspect-Based Sentiment Lexicon}
\label{sec:method-aspects}

We built a lexicon of 27 aspects covering the functional and experiential
surface of devotional apps (prayer-time accuracy, adhan reminders, Quran audio,
qibla, prayer tracking, advertising, monetisation, and others), comprising 340
keywords and 106 regular-expression patterns applied at the sentence level.
Each sentence can carry zero, one, or multiple tags. Keyword and pattern counts
vary widely across aspects, and since several results turn on how thinly an
aspect is defined, we release the lexicon in full with the aspect groupings
each research question composes from it (\S\ref{sec:method-repro}).

\subsection{Affect Lexicon}
\label{sec:method-affect}

RQ1 additionally uses two small keyword lists, one guilt- and anxiety-related
and one motivation-related, to compare affective register inside and outside
tracker discourse. Alone among our measurements these carry no gold sample and
no precision figure: the 300-row aspect draw and the 500-document sentiment set
label aspects and valence, not the guilt/motivation distinction. We release
both lists, report what they produce as a lexical-frequency comparison rather
than a validated measurement of guilt (\S\ref{sec:rq1}), and rest the construct
claim on the hand-coded passes.

\subsection{Devotional-Formula Filtering}
\label{sec:method-devotional-filtering}

After lexicon tagging, we removed aspect assignments whose triggering sentence
consists of devotional formulae rather than substantive feedback: 1,686 of the
192,874 aspect-tagged sentence pairs the lexicon produced (0.9\%), leaving
191,188. The unit is the (aspect, sentence) pair, so a sentence carrying two
tags counts twice. The step is necessary because general-purpose English
feature vocabulary misfires badly on devotional register. The clearest case is
\texttt{goal\_system}, at 0\% precision against its gold rows because reviewers
routinely close a review with phrases such as ``May Allah reward you,'' which
goal-related keywords match as if they described a tracking feature. This is a
substantive finding rather than a cleaning footnote: aspect tooling validated
on general product review text does not transfer cleanly to religious register,
and a devotional-formula pass is necessary before aspect-level analysis is
trustworthy here.

\subsection{Demand Mining}
\label{sec:method-demand}

We extracted five types of demand signal by pattern matching, each attached to
the aspect(s) it co-occurs with: \emph{request} (explicit asks),
\emph{absence} (noting a feature is missing), \emph{churn} (a missing feature
as a reason for leaving), \emph{preference} (a feature as the reason for
choosing an app), and \emph{tenure} (a feature cited in long-term use). Against
a 200-row gold sample stratified 40 rows per type, overall precision was 91.5\%
(183/200): preference 100\%, tenure 100\%, churn 97.5\%, request 95\%, absence
65\%.

A provenance caveat applies. The gold sample was labelled from one run of the
extractor; an upstream threshold then changed, the extractor was re-run, and
the regenerated output no longer matches it row-for-row (only 11 of the 200
labelled reviewIds recur). Demand counts reported elsewhere come from the
current extraction, while 91.5\% was measured on its predecessor. We report it
as the best available estimate of demand-pattern precision, not as a
measurement of the exact rows underlying every count.

\subsection{Promise Extraction}
\label{sec:method-promise}

To compare what an application promises against what it delivers, we extracted
claimed features from each store description and compared them against a
hand-built feature matrix (one row per app, one column per aspect). Agreement
is weak: mean Cohen's \(\kappa = 0.272\) across 20 features, maximum 0.62 for
women's tracking, and four features at \(\kappa = 0.00\) --- travel
concession, calendar integration, prayer times by location, timely reminders.
Figure~\ref{fig:promise-agreement} gives the per-feature breakdown, including
where the description misses a feature the matrix records and where it
overclaims one it does not.

Those four zeros are prevalence artefacts: one source records the feature in
almost every application or almost none, leaving too little variance for
\(\kappa\) to correct against, so it collapses despite high raw agreement,
96.2\% for timely reminders (26 of 26 against 25 of 26) and 96.2\% for travel
concession. Mean raw agreement across the 20 features is 73.1\%. We report
both, since \(\kappa\) alone understates how far the sources coincide on the
features RQ6a and RQ3 depend on, while raw agreement alone flatters those where
they diverge.

\subsection{Topic Modelling}
\label{sec:method-topics}

We fit a BERTopic model over \(C_{\mathrm{en}}\) (276,422 documents) using a
seeded UMAP reduction and a stopword list extended with Islamic-domain terms
and application-name tokens, to keep generic devotional vocabulary and app
names from dominating topic keywords. The model produced 40 topics plus an
outlier bucket. Topic 0 is an undifferentiated generic-praise cluster (168,054
documents, 61\% of modelled documents) and the outlier bucket holds 62,745
(22.7\%); the remaining 39 topics, which we label and report, cover about
45,600 documents (16\% of \(C_{\mathrm{en}}\)). All 63 topics across this model
and three targeted sub-models were labelled by hand across four sheets,
alongside each model's outlier bucket. The topic model therefore characterises
the interpretable minority of the corpus, not the corpus as a whole.

The three sub-models are fit on aspect-restricted subsets for RQ1 (tracker
discourse), RQ4 (menstrual and inclusion discourse), and RQ5 (bloat discourse).
Topic structure under BERTopic is seed-sensitive; we refit each across five
seeds fixed in advance (42--46), before inspecting any result.
\texttt{rq1\_tracker} is stable (mean pairwise Adjusted Rand Index 0.837);
\texttt{rq5\_bloat} is not (6--11 topics, mean pairwise ARI 0.452), so where we
draw on it we claim recurring themes rather than a fixed partition.

\subsection{Statistical Models}
\label{sec:method-models}

We fit five models (M1--M5), one primary per research question for RQ1--RQ5:
M1 (RQ2, competing costs of accuracy and interface complaints), M2 (RQ1,
sentiment by tracker tier), M3 (RQ4, inclusion), M4 (RQ5, bloat and hardware),
M5 (RQ3, competitive edge features). RQ6 is answered descriptively, comparing
promised features against the sentiment their mentions record, and enters no
inferential model. Each primary specification is a review-level mixed-effects
model with a random intercept per application, accounting for reviews
clustering within 26 apps of very different size. Where a mixed model did not
converge we report a cluster-robust OLS fit on the same data and specification,
clustering standard errors on application.

Coefficients from the primary specifications enter a single Benjamini--Hochberg
false-discovery-rate correction: 29 in total, of which 20 survive at
\(q < 0.05\). Cluster-robust refits are not entered, since correcting a
robustness check against the test it checks would double-count the contrast;
the eight ordinal refits of M1's complaint terms \emph{are}, because they
change the outcome variable rather than only the error structure. The asymmetry
is a defensible line but not the only one, and a reader who would draw it
elsewhere can recompute from the released coefficient table.

Eleven terms cannot support inference, and we report all eleven. Eight are
mixed-model terms whose standard errors are degenerate rather than merely large
--- M2's two tracker-tier terms and six from M4, with standard errors from
\(4.1\times10^{2}\) to \(2.0\times10^{6}\) and every one reporting
\(p \approx 0.99999\). These carry \(q\)-values and sit inside the 29, so in
isolation they resemble ``tested, not significant'' rather than ``failed to
converge''; they are eight of the nine terms failing correction. The ninth is
\texttt{madhab} in the ordinal specification, which returns a small, imprecise
estimate reversing sign against M1 (\S\ref{sec:rq2}). The other three --- M3's
\texttt{women\_feature\_score\_diff}, M5's \texttt{has\_mosque\_finder}, and
one M5 demand term --- return neither a standard error nor a \(p\)-value, so
they fall outside the 29 and no correction applies. The cause is structural: a
random intercept per app, fitted over 26 apps, cannot be identified separately
from an app-level predictor in the same model. We cite null results only from
models that converged, and never describe one of these eleven as evidence of no
effect.

\subsection{Measurement Quality}
\label{sec:method-measurement}

Because several claims rest on aspect tags, we report their precision directly.
Against a 300-row gold sample, of which 192 rows still match under the current
lexicon configuration, unweighted aspect precision is 69.3\% and
corpus-volume-weighted precision is 84.4\% (95\% CI [79.7, 88.9]). The gold set
was originally labelled against an earlier, broader configuration including a
zero-shot backstop; every subsequent lexicon change has only removed matches,
so the 192 surviving rows are an unbiased subset of the original 300 rather
than a re-labelling exercise. Two aspects, \texttt{calendar\_sync} and \texttt{goal\_system}, lost all their
gold rows to that narrowing (together 0.2\% of aspect-tagged volume) and are
excluded from the weighted figure.

That figure is driven by high-volume aspects and conceals substantial
variation. The gold draw sampled all 27 aspects at 11 rows each;
Table~\ref{tab:aspect-precision} reports the 25 whose rows survive. Seven fall
below 50\%, with gold samples as small as one or two rows, and several are
load-bearing: every count drawn from one of the seven is reported elsewhere as
a precision-adjusted estimate with its interval. Seventeen sit at or above 64\%
and supply almost all model input, with two exceptions. \texttt{madhab}, at
60\%, enters M1 and is also the one M1 term reversing sign under the ordinal
check, so we rest no directional claim on it (\S\ref{sec:rq2}). RQ3's
mosque-finder contrast is fit on the subset the \texttt{mosque\_finder} tagger
selects, at 27\%; that noise falls on both arms, so we read the direction of
the gap and not its magnitude (\S\ref{sec:rq3}). No other model term is drawn
from an aspect below 50\%.

\subsection{Research Ethics and Data Handling}
\label{sec:method-ethics}

The corpus is public store review text. No user was recruited, contacted, or
observed, and no account-linked or private data was accessed, so the study
involved no human participants and was not submitted for human-subjects review.
We none the less treat it as data about people: the reviews concern religious
observance, and some disclose menstruation, illness, or lapses in practice,
which a reviewer complaining about a notification may not have understood
themselves to be publishing. We therefore report no reviewer names or
identifiers, attribute quotations only to an application and a star rating, and
choose quotations carrying no incidental personal detail. We quote verbatim,
since altering wording in a study whose claim concerns devotional register
would misrepresent the evidence, and accept the residual risk that a short
quoted string can be matched back through store search. Two quotations disclose
menstruation, the most sensitive category above; we judged this necessary, since
RQ4's claim is that users voice the need explicitly and paraphrase would convert
direct evidence into our own assertion of it. We name applications throughout,
including in criticism: they are public commercial products, and the argument
cannot be made without saying which did what. Where we name one in connection
with a specific controversy rather than an ecosystem pattern, we confine the
claim to that episode (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Use of AI Assistance}
\label{sec:method-ai}

All analysis software was written with the assistance of Claude Code,
Anthropic's command-line coding agent, driven interactively by the authors. We
disclose this because the assistance was substantive in the conduct of the
research, not confined to preparing the manuscript. Three boundaries bound what
it can threaten. First, no generative model produced, imputed, translated, or
altered any review text, any label used as data, or any reported statistic.
Second, the design is the authors' own: the six research questions were
specified in a written analysis plan committed to version control before the
analysis was run, the aspect lexicon and coding frame were hand-built, and every
gold-standard label was assigned by a human annotator without model assistance.
Third, the authors read, executed, and are responsible for all code.

Assistant-written code can carry defects a human author would not introduce.
Our safeguards are those already reported: per-stage precision against
hand-labelled gold sets, a component those checks rejected at 11.5\% precision,
the seed-stability pass, and the byte-identical re-run below. One defect was
found and fixed: the three sub-models shared a hardcoded output path and
overwrote the ecosystem topic sheet on each run. The sheet was rebuilt from the
saved model and stored assignments without refitting, and no reported statistic
depended on it.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:method-repro}

The analysis was run on 2026-09-03 at commit \texttt{5da2db7}; re-running the
modelling stage from a clean tree at the same commit gave byte-identical
output. All stochastic components are seeded, and the five seeds used for the
topic-stability check (42--46) were fixed in advance of inspecting any result,
so the seed set could not be adjusted after the fact. A run manifest recording
commit, hardware, batch size, and library versions for every reported figure
accompanies the artefact.

\paragraph{Data and code availability.} We release the full pipeline, the
aspect lexicon, the feature matrix, every hand-labelled gold and topic sheet,
the model output, and the run manifest, at a URL supplied on acceptance and
withheld here for anonymous review, together with derived data sufficient to
recompute every table and figure. Two things are excluded: the raw review
corpus, which the store's terms do not permit us to redistribute and which
carries the re-identification risk noted in \S\ref{sec:method-ethics}, and the
fitted model binaries, which are large and reproducible from the seeded
pipeline. The gold sheets carry the review text their labels apply to, since a
precision figure cannot be checked without it; that is a few hundred rows drawn
for verification rather than the corpus, and we judge the disclosure
proportionate on the reasoning governing quotation above. The collection script
and application list are included, so the corpus can be re-gathered, though
store histories change and exact reconstruction is not possible.
