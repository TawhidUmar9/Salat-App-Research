\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

We state these plainly rather than as hedges: each has a claim ceiling the
results above already respect.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{No users were studied.} Every finding is inferred from public
app-store reviews, a self-selected sample skewed toward strong positive and
strong negative experiences and at best an indirect proxy for lived religious
practice. Every claim about user experience here should be read as a claim
about what reviewers chose to write down.

\item \textbf{The corpus is an English/US-locale slice, and the applications
were chosen purposively.} Reviews were requested with \texttt{lang=en} and
\texttt{country=us} (\S\ref{sec:method-corpus}), so the corpus under-represents
the regions where most of the world's Muslims live. The aspects most exposed to
this are the regionally patterned ones --- madhab, calculation method, and
travel concession --- and RQ2's finding that madhab complaints are mild and
cheap should be read with that in mind rather than as a global claim. The 26
applications were also selected by judgement rather than by an exhaustive or
random procedure, and no target sample size was set in advance. Because every
application was collected under identical parameters, this limits how far the
findings generalise rather than threatening the comparisons between them.

\item \textbf{Aspect-tagging precision is uneven.} Seven of the 25 aspects with
surviving gold rows fall below 50\%, two of them at 0\%
(\S\ref{sec:method-measurement}). Where we report a count for one of those seven we pair it with a
precision-adjusted estimate and rest no argument on the raw figure. The five
statistical models draw on aspects at 64--100\% precision with two exceptions
flagged at the point of use: \texttt{madhab} at 60\% enters M1, and RQ3's
mosque-finder contrast is fit on a subset a 27\%-precision tagger selects; measurement noise of this
kind attenuates effects toward zero rather than manufacturing them, so it works
against, not for, the paper's claims.

\item \textbf{Eleven model terms cannot support inference.} Eight are
non-converged rather than precisely estimated nulls; we retain those eight in
the Benjamini--Hochberg denominator of 29 for transparency, and they are eight
of the nine terms there that fail to survive correction. The other three return
neither a standard error nor a \(p\)-value and therefore sit outside that
denominator altogether (\S\ref{sec:method-models}). Every null reported in this
paper is cited from a fit that converged.

\item \textbf{The topic model characterises a minority of the corpus.} 22.7\%
of modelled documents fall in the outlier bucket and 61\% in a single
undifferentiated praise cluster; the 39 topics we label cover about 16\%.
Topic-model findings describe the interpretable minority of reviews, not the
corpus.

\item \textbf{Topic structure is sensitive to seed.} The \texttt{rq5\_bloat}
sub-model yields between 6 and 11 topics across five fixed seeds (mean pairwise
ARI 0.452). We claim the recurring themes such a model surfaces, not a specific
partition.

\item \textbf{The RQ4 sub-model collapsed around one application's privacy
episode.} Six of ten topics sit on a single theme concerning one application's
user data being sold to US military-linked buyers, holding 58\% of the
sub-model's non-outlier documents. That application is Muslim Pro, whose
November 2020 data-sale reporting we name explicitly because the finding is
specific to that episode rather than a property of the ecosystem; it precludes
further resolution of menstrual-handling discourse (\S\ref{sec:rq4}).

\item \textbf{Illustrative quotations skew toward one application.} Seven of the
13 reviews quoted in this paper come from Athan, the corpus's largest
tracker-bearing application, because quotation followed the volume of available
text rather than a sampling rule. This is the same hazard we identify as a
methodological contribution (\S\ref{sec:rq1-coding}), so we name it here: the
quotations illustrate claims, and every claim they illustrate rests on the
corpus-scale or cross-application evidence reported alongside them, never on
the quoted reviews themselves.

\item \textbf{The affect lexicon is unvalidated.} The guilt and motivation
keyword lists behind RQ1's lexical comparison have no gold sample and no
precision figure, and their vocabulary overlaps ordinary praise and defect
language (\S\ref{sec:method-affect}). We report that comparison as a fact
about two lexicons and rest RQ1's claim about guilt on the hand-coded passes,
which point the same way.

\item \textbf{Two qualitative coding passes, never pooled.} The first (50
reviews) is drawn from a single application through a tie-break artefact in the
selection rule; the second (30 reviews) corrects this, stratified across the
remaining 22 tracker applications (\S\ref{sec:rq1-coding}). Together they code
80 reviews. We treat this as supporting evidence that sharpens the
corpus-scale results, not as a primary contribution.

\item \textbf{Three small-\(N\) case studies are descriptive only.} Inclusion
features ship in 3 of 26 applications; companion hardware and qasr travel
concession each ship in exactly 1. None supports a between-app statistical
estimate, and we report none.

\item \textbf{Promise-source agreement is weak.} Store descriptions and the
hand-built feature matrix agree at a mean Cohen's \(\kappa\) of 0.272, against
73.1\% mean raw agreement; four features return \(\kappa = 0\) as a prevalence
artefact rather than through disagreement (\S\ref{sec:method-promise}). Any claim depending on what an application
promises should be read with this ceiling in mind.

\item \textbf{The demand-precision sample predates the final extraction.} The
200-row gold sample behind the 91.5\% figure was labelled against an earlier
run; current demand counts come from the regenerated extraction
(\S\ref{sec:method-demand}).

\item \textbf{Topic modelling covers English-identified text only.} Of 324,687
reviews carrying text, fastText identified 276,422 as English, and only this
subset entered topic modelling. Topic-model findings do not characterise
discourse in the languages this filter excluded.

\item \textbf{Reviews are cross-sectional, not longitudinal.} We treat every
review as an independent observation. Nothing links a user's reviews across
time, so we cannot speak to how an individual's relationship with an
application changes over their tenure, only to aggregate patterns.

\end{itemize}
