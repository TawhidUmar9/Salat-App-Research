\section{Design Implications}
\label{sec:design-implications}

We state these at the level of the ecosystem, and we do not recommend adding
features generically: the paper's central finding is that feature count is not
the lever satisfaction runs on. Each recommendation is driven by RQ6b's
unmet-need ranking or by a specific reported result, and every coverage
recommendation is paired with its delivery point, since the two gaps require
different remedies.

\subsection{Deliver What Is Already Promised, Before Adding Anything}

The two most-broken promises are also the two most basic: adhan reminders
(broken in 5 of 20 promising applications) and monetisation terms (5 of 16)
(\S\ref{sec:rq6}). Prompting failure is independently the dominant tracker
complaint, at 46\% of the cross-app coding pass (\S\ref{sec:rq1}). These are
not independent problems: an application that cannot reliably fire a
notification cannot support a prayer-tracking practice built on top of it,
whatever else it ships. Reliable delivery of the core promise is the
precondition for everything else on this list, not one item alongside them.

\subsection{Treat Prayer-Time Correctness as a Reliability Requirement}

Prayer-time accuracy costs \(-1.112\) stars, the second-costliest of the complaint
aspects we model, and 41.6\% of accuracy complaints arrive inside four- or five-star
reviews (\S\ref{sec:rq2}). A team prioritising defects from its own rating
distribution will systematically under-prioritise this class of failure. We
recommend instrumenting for accuracy defects directly, comparing computed times
against an authoritative reference and logging location and calculation-method
edge cases, rather than inferring severity from ratings that conceal them.

\subsection{Accommodate Practice, Rather Than Softening Mechanics}
\label{sec:design-accommodate}

Where tracker dissatisfaction carries a normative complaint, it is that the app
mis-models worship, not that its mechanics apply too much pressure
(\S\ref{sec:rq1}). Menstrual handling makes this concrete: it ranks second on
the unmet-need index, is absent from 23 of 26 applications, and in the three
that do ship it, sentiment on the topic is no better than in applications
without it (\S\ref{sec:rq4}). This is simultaneously a coverage problem and a
quality problem, and closing the coverage gap without fixing reliability would
not resolve it.

\subsection{Surface Less; Do Not Necessarily Contain Less}

The lever available to a design team is what the interface surfaces in a given
moment, not how many capabilities the application contains
(\S\ref{sec:rq5}). A full-featured application with a disciplined interface is
not the problem the bloat framing warns against; a lean application that
surfaces its few features poorly can still earn the complaint.

\subsection{The Unserved Categories}

Four categories fall below a quarter of ecosystem coverage against demonstrated
demand (\S\ref{sec:rq6}). \textbf{Companion hardware} tops the ranking, absent
from 25 of 26 applications; sentiment toward hardware is in fact \emph{better}
inside the one application shipping a device than in the request-driven
mentions elsewhere (\S\ref{sec:rq5}), so this is demand rather than
dissatisfaction with an existing product. \textbf{Menstrual accommodation}
ranks second, per \S\ref{sec:design-accommodate}. \textbf{Travel concession}
(\emph{qasr}) has essentially no market presence, one application in 26,
against a small but specific stream of requests (\S\ref{sec:rq3}).
\textbf{Calendar integration} rounds out the four. None of these is a call to
add features indiscriminately; each is a demand-evidenced gap in an ecosystem
whose more pressing problem remains delivering what it has already promised.
