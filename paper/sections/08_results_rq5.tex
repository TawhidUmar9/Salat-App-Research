\section{RQ5: Feature Bloat and Companion Hardware}
\label{sec:rq5}

A common claim holds that adding features creates clutter complaints, and that
leaner apps are more satisfying. We tested this directly, on a hypothesis
pre-specified on 2026-08-26, before running the full corpus. It fails in both
directions: feature count does not predict bloat complaints, and if anything
the relationship runs backward. What users call ``bloat'' turns out to be
about the interface, not the feature count.

\subsection{Feature Count Does Not Predict Bloat Complaints}

The primary mixed-effects model (M4) did not converge on its
\texttt{feature\_count} term, so we report the cluster-robust sensitivity fit:
the interaction between bloat complaints and feature count gives
\(\beta=-0.000487\) (95\% CI \([-0.00136, 0.00038]\), \(p=0.272\)), clustered
on 26 apps. Adding features does not make users more likely to call an app
bloated. Nor is this a popularity artefact: feature count correlates only
weakly with install count across the 26 apps (Spearman \(\rho=0.244\)).

\subsection{If Anything, It Runs Backward}

Figure~\ref{fig:rq5-bloat-curve} shows the opposite of what the bloat
hypothesis predicts: the five leanest apps (6--9 features) average 3.96 stars
at a 5.2\% bloat-complaint rate; the five fullest (13--14 features) average
4.71 stars at 1.6\% --- 3.3 times lower. The pattern survives dropping the two
smallest apps by review volume, and the clearest single pair is iPray (8
features, 8.0\% bloat rate) against Athan (13 features, 1.5\%). An exploratory
app-level correlation, entered into the correction and surviving it, points
the same way: feature count correlates positively with mean sentiment
(\(\rho=+0.471\), \(p=.015\)) and mean score (\(\rho=+0.422\), \(p=.032\)).

\subsection{Bloat Complaints Are Interface Complaints}

So what drives them? A review complaining about bloat is 23.98 times more
likely than base rate to also complain about the interface (Fisher exact
OR \(=29.41\), \(p=7.4\times10^{-137}\)). Even under the most conservative
reading --- restricting to the 40 reviews that keep the two complaints in
separate sentences, rather than fusing them into one phrase --- the lift is
still 7.62 times (OR \(=8.78\), \(p=9.3\times10^{-24}\)). Users often say it
as one thing:

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``Widget takes up too much space, [5\texttimes{}2], but displays
information only in [4\texttimes{}1]. Remaining blocks are blank, unusable for
other apps, just hogging space. Otherwise, good app, used daily. Has more than
timetables which for me is just unimportant clutter.''
\end{quote}
\hspace*{1em}--- Athan: Prayer Times \& Al Quran, 4 stars

A separate topic model fit on bloat-tagged reviews alone agrees, from a
different angle: its top topics are dominated by users \emph{praising}
simplicity (minimal UI, no ads), with clutter, advertising, and slowness on
the negative side. Users are evaluating how much the interface shows them, not
how many features the app contains.

\subsection{Companion Hardware: A Case of One}

One app, iQIBLA Life, ships a companion hardware device. Sentiment toward
hardware runs \emph{better} inside that app (37.7\% negative) than across the
23 of the 25 apps without one that mention hardware at all, where mentions are
almost entirely unmet requests for a watch or ring (49.2\% negative). The \texttt{companion\_hardware} tagger runs
at 40\% precision, so these figures rest on tags of that quality. Shipping the
hardware is associated with better sentiment, not worse --- the opposite of a
bloat story, and consistent with RQ3: unmet requests read more positively than
usage reports of something that can fail.

The feature-bloat hypothesis fails in either direction. Feature count is
uncorrelated with bloat complaints, and the leanest apps draw them at over
three times the rate of the fullest. What users call bloat is a property of
the interface rather than a count of capabilities.
