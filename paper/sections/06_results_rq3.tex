\section{RQ3: Travel and Competitive Edge}
\label{sec:rq3}

A common claim in mobile-app design is that distinctive features --- a mosque
finder, a travel prayer-shortening (\emph{qasr}) mode --- win and keep users.
RQ3 tests this against what users actually cite as reasons for loyalty.
Neither feature supports the differentiation story: what earns loyalty here is
reliable core function, not travel support.

\subsection{What Users Actually Cite}

Across 4,696 explicit preference citations (``that's why I use this app''),
the top aspects are adhan reminders (374), Quran audio (293), prayer-time
accuracy (290), qibla (155), and adhkar, or remembrance prayers (152). Mosque finder does not make the
top eight; qasr does not appear at all. Preference rates are nearly identical
whether an app ships a mosque finder (1.38\%) or not (1.24\%).

\subsection{Mosque Finder's Coefficient Runs Backward, and Why}

A cluster-robust fit on reviews mentioning mosque finder gives
\(\beta=-0.137\) (\(p=0.003\), \(n=3{,}010\)): apps that \emph{have} the
feature draw more negative mentions of it, not fewer (27.0\% versus 23.0\%).
The tagger is noisy (27\% precision, 95\% CI [10, 57]), so we read only the
direction of the effect, not its size. The direction has a simple explanation:
apps without the feature mostly get \emph{requests} (``please add nearby
mosques,'' 243 signals, mostly neutral-to-positive), while apps that ship it
get \emph{usage reports} of a feature that sometimes fails, which read
negative. A user can only complain about a feature that exists. The gap
measures wishing for a feature versus using one, not the cost of shipping it.
The coefficient is also unstable across specifications --- the mixed-effects
fit on the same contrast returns an undefined standard error --- so we rest no
directional claim about competitive advantage on it.

\subsection{Qasr: Demand Without a Test}

Only one app of 26, \emph{Muslim Bangla Quran Hadith Dua}, implements qasr, so
no between-app comparison is possible --- this is demand evidence, not a
causal test. Demand is real but modest: 21 signals total (10 requests, 5
preference citations, 3 statements of absence, 3 churn statements), mostly
from apps that lack the feature:

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``My second suggestion is that prayer times automatically adjust when
I'm traveling because man forgets and my prayers be off.''
\end{quote}
\hspace*{1em}--- Muslim Pro: Quran Athan Prayer, 4 stars

A broader aspect tag for qasr matches 637 mentions but scored 0 of 2 on its
gold rows and is evidently catching general travel and pilgrimage discourse
rather than qasr specifically, so we do not report it as a measure of qasr
volume and rest the argument on the 21 hand-read demand signals.

Where travel failures do appear, the pattern is prayer times not updating when
location changes:

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``...it doesn't seem to automatically change your location when
traveling until you physically refresh the prayer times page. Unfortunately
today I accidentally broke my fast 1 minute early b/c the location was still
set to an area almost 1 hour away, pls fix this inshaAllah.''
\end{quote}
\hspace*{1em}--- Athan: Prayer Times \& Al Quran, 4 stars

Neither travel-oriented feature confers a competitive edge. What users do
cite --- adhan reminders, Quran audio, prayer-time accuracy --- points back to
core functions, and those are themselves among RQ6's most-broken promises
(\S\ref{sec:rq6}). Competitive advantage here looks like doing the basics
reliably.
