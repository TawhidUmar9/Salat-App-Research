\section{RQ3: Travel and Competitive Edge}
\label{sec:rq3}

A third framing common in mobile-app HCI research is that differentiating
features win and retain users --- that a distinctive capability such as a
mosque finder or a travel prayer-shortening (\emph{qasr}) mode confers a
competitive edge. RQ3 tests this against what users cite as reasons for
loyalty, and against the sentiment record of apps shipping these two
travel-oriented features. Neither supports the differentiation story: what
earns loyalty here is the reliability of core functions.

\subsection{What Users Cite Is Not Travel Support}

Across 4,696 preference citations --- reviews explicitly stating why the user
chose or kept an app, in the register of ``that's why I use this'' or ``only
app that'' --- the aspects named most are adhan reminders (374), Quran audio
(293), prayer-time accuracy (290), qibla (155), and adhkar (152). Mosque finder
does not appear in the top eight cited aspects, and qasr does not appear at
all. Preference-citation rates are near-identical whether an app ships a mosque
finder or not: 1.38\% of reviews for the 12 apps that have one, against 1.24\%
for the 14 that do not.

\subsection{The Mosque-Finder Coefficient Runs the Wrong Way, and the Reason Matters}

A cluster-robust fit on reviews mentioning mosque finder gives
\(\beta = -0.137\) (\(p = 0.003\), 21 app clusters, \(n = 3{,}010\)): apps that
\emph{have} a mosque finder attract more negative mentions of it, not fewer ---
27.0\% negative against 23.0\% in apps without it, positive mentions moving the
other way (38.6\% against 43.6\%). The \texttt{mosque\_finder} tagger runs at
27\% precision (95\% CI [10, 57]), so of the 3,010 mentions entering this
comparison, drawn from 3,043 tagged corpus-wide, roughly 830 are genuine
(interval [297, 1{,}721]); we report neither figure unqualified. The noise
falls on both arms, so the \emph{direction} of the gap is more trustworthy than
its \emph{size}.

That direction is not the feature harming apps that ship it, because the two
groups of mentions are not comparable speech acts. Of the 14 applications
without a mosque finder, 11 draw any mention of one, overwhelmingly as
\emph{requests} --- 243 demand signals across 9 apps --- which phrased as
``please add nearby mosques'' read neutral-to-positive. Of the 12 that have the
feature, 10 draw mentions, and there they are \emph{usage reports}; a usage
report of a feature that fails to work reads negative. Those 21 applications
are the clusters in the model. A user can only complain about a feature that
exists, so the gap measures the difference between wishing for a feature and
using one, not the cost of shipping it.

The coefficient is also unstable across specifications: the mixed-effects fit
on the same contrast returns \(+0.05\) with an undefined (NaN) standard error
and so cannot arbitrate. We treat the cluster-robust estimate as the more
trustworthy, but rest no claim about competitive advantage on a coefficient
that reverses sign between specifications, one of which produced no error
estimate at all.

\subsection{Qasr Travel Concession: Demand Evidence, Not a Causal Test}

Exactly one application of the 26, \emph{Muslim Bangla Quran Hadith Dua},
implements qasr (the Islamic travel concession shortening obligatory prayers).
With \(N = 1\) no between-app comparison is available, so we treat qasr as
demand evidence rather than a test of any causal claim. Demand is real if
modest: 21 signals --- 10 requests, 5 preference citations, 3 statements of
absence, 3 churn statements --- of which 20 come from 5 of the 25 apps lacking
the feature. The requests are specific and practical:

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``My second suggestion is that prayer times automatically adjust when
I'm traveling because man forgets and my prayers be off.''
\end{quote}
\hspace*{1em}--- Muslim Pro: Quran Athan Prayer, 4 stars

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``please add namaz qasar for all 5 prayers \ldots\ while anyone is out
of city or in traveling''
\end{quote}
\hspace*{1em}--- Prayer Times - Qibla \& Namaz, 5 stars

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``I wish it was installed prior my hajj trip''
\end{quote}
\hspace*{1em}--- Athan: Prayer Times \& Al Quran, 5 stars

A caveat applies to the broader \texttt{qasr\_travel} tag, distinct from those
21 hand-read signals. It matches 637 mentions across 18 apps and runs 76.7\%
positive against 7.1\% negative --- implausible for a capability 25 of 26 apps
lack --- and scored 0 of 2 on its gold rows, with only 3.3\% of matches being
demand signals. The lexicon is catching travel and pilgrimage discourse (hajj,
umrah, journeys) rather than qasr functionality. We therefore never report 637
as qasr discussion volume, and rest the argument on the 21 demand signals, read
directly rather than inferred from lexicon matches.

\subsection{Dominant Travel Failure Mode}

Where travel-related failures appear, the recurring pattern is prayer times
failing to adjust automatically when location changes, discovered only after
the user acts on a stale time:

\begin{quote}
\itshape ``The only thing that I don't like about this app is it doesn't seem
to automatically change your location when traveling until you physically
refresh the prayer times page. Unfortunately today I accidentally broke my
fast 1 minute early b/c the location was still set to an area almost 1 hour
away, pls fix this inshaAllah.''
\end{quote}
\hspace*{1em}--- Athan: Prayer Times \& Al Quran, 4 stars
