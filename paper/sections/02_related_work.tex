\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

We draw on four strands: religious technology in HCI, gamification and
self-tracking, app-store review mining as a method, and the literature on
feature bloat.

\subsection{Religious and Devotional Technology}

HCI and CSCW have studied religious practice across several traditions.
\citet{abokhodair2020holytweets} analyse 2.6 million tweets sharing Quran
verses, combining topic analysis with a qualitative study of devotional goals.
\citet{wolf2023blessing} design for ``uncontrollability'' in a Christian
blessing ritual, and \citet{bhattacharjee2025residual} interview Hindu
migrants sustaining practice across displacement. None addresses prayer-time
apps specifically or reviews at ecosystem scale.

Closer to our topic, \citet{bellar2017ipray} analyses 65 Catholic and Islamic
prayer-app descriptions and finds reminder features central to design ---
which our RQ3 and RQ6 corroborate independently at a much larger scale.
\citet{azahari2025comparative} compare two named apps by expert evaluation,
finding intrusive ads in the feature-rich one against simplicity in the
minimal one. Our RQ5 revisits that pattern with corpus evidence and
complicates it: we do not find that a fuller feature set predicts worse
sentiment.

The closest methodological precedent is a pair of studies by
\citet{noor2025halaltourism} and \citet{noor2026halalsuperapps}, who apply
content analysis to reviews of one Muslim-lifestyle app and find themes ---
prayer-time accuracy, adhan reliability, advertising --- that recur throughout
our own results. Both studies analyse a single app (279 and 2,459 reviews);
ours analyses 26 apps and 732,194 reviews with aspect-based sentiment and
mixed-effects models, which is what lets us tell an ecosystem-level finding
apart from an artefact of one app's release history (\S\ref{sec:rq1-coding}).

\subsection{Gamification and Self-Tracking}

A substantial literature documents that gamified self-tracking --- streaks,
points, badges --- can produce real harm. \citet{renfree2016dontkick} show that
streaks sustain new behaviour but create a dependency whose loss introduces
fragility. In interview studies of wearable-tracker users,
\citet{humphreys2026toxic} and \citet{demetrovics2025lifestylepressures}
report anxiety, guilt, and loss of autonomy, and \citet{bai2024darkside} find
competition-based gamification increases stress. Against this, a meta-analysis
by \citet{six2021gamification} finds no difference in outcomes between
gamified and non-gamified mental-health apps, so the harm is not universal.

RQ1 asks whether the same mechanism operates when the tracked behaviour is a
religious obligation rather than a personal fitness goal, and finds that it
largely does not (\S\ref{sec:rq1}). We read this as a boundary condition rather
than a refutation: the harm this literature documents may depend on the
tracked behaviour being one the user set for themselves, which a prayer
obligation is not.

\subsection{App-Store Review Mining as Method}

Mining app-store reviews is an established method.
\citet{dabrowski2022analysing} survey 182 papers on the field, and
\citet{ciurumelea2017analyzing} build a review taxonomy across 39 apps.

The closest precedent for our scale is \citet{haggag2022mhealth}, who extract
over five million reviews for 278 mHealth apps and classify them into 14
aspect categories. We follow their basic move --- ecosystem-scale mining
resolved to named aspects --- but depart on one point that matters for RQ2:
several of their findings are stated as shares of users awarding one star to an
aspect, treating the star rating as a readable index of severity. Our corpus
does not support that assumption (\S\ref{sec:rq2}).
\citet{guluzade2024eating} pair a functionality audit of 27 eating-disorder
apps with a content analysis of reviews; their two-source design, a matrix read
against what reviewers say, is structurally our promise-versus-delivery
comparison (\S\ref{sec:method-promise}), and the nearest analogue we have found
to RQ4's menstrual-exemption gap outside a religious setting.

Beyond the devotional domain, this paper contributes two risks that this
literature has not, to our knowledge, quantified directly: star ratings
systematically hide a class of substantive complaint (\S\ref{sec:rq2}), and
single-app qualitative sampling can manufacture themes that read as
genre-level findings (\S\ref{sec:rq1-coding}).

\subsection{Feature Bloat and Interface Complexity}

That more features degrade user experience is widely assumed and rarely
tested. \citet{marzi2022overfeaturing} reviews the ``over-featuring''
literature and finds the phenomenon ranked among the top risks of
new-product-development failure, alongside a lack of investigation of its
causes. \citet{blackler2016rtfm} find over-featured products do produce
negative emotional experiences, and that most users do not exercise most
features. RQ5 tests the commonly assumed causal link --- that a higher feature
count predicts more bloat complaints --- directly against corpus data, and
finds it fails in both directions (\S\ref{sec:rq5}).
