\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

We draw on four strands: religious and devotional technology in HCI;
gamification and self-tracking, specifically the tracking-induced guilt
literature RQ1 contradicts; app-store review mining as a method; and the
feature-bloat literature RQ5 tests.

\subsection{Religious and Devotional Technology in HCI}

HCI and CSCW have taken up religious practice as a first-class design concern
across several traditions and platforms. \citet{abokhodair2020holytweets}
analyse 2.6 million tweets sharing Quran verses on Arabic Twitter, combining
topic analysis with a qualitative study of devotional goals and demonstrating
that large-scale mixed-methods analysis of Islamic digital practice is feasible
--- a precedent our review corpus follows on a different platform.
\citet{wolf2023blessing} design for ``uncontrollability'' in a Christian
blessing ritual, a value in tension with the on-demand paradigms most software
assumes, and \citet{bhattacharjee2025residual} interview Hindu migrants in
Canada sustaining practice across displacement. None addresses prayer-time or
devotional utility apps specifically, or reviews at ecosystem scale.

Closer to our object, \citet{bellar2017ipray} textually analyses 65 Catholic
and Islamic prayer-app descriptions and user-tests one app from each tradition,
identifying the centrality of reminder features --- which our RQ3 and RQ6
corroborate independently at a different scale.
\citet{azahari2025comparative} compare Muslim Pro and Sajda by expert
heuristic evaluation, finding intrusive advertising and complex navigation in
the feature-rich app against simplicity and limited functionality in the
minimal one. RQ5 revisits that pattern with corpus evidence rather than expert
judgement, and complicates it: we do not find that a fuller feature set
predicts worse sentiment.

The closest methodological precedent is a pair of studies by
\citet{noor2025halaltourism} and \citet{noor2026halalsuperapps}, applying
Leximancer content analysis to reviews of a single Muslim-lifestyle
application and identifying service-quality themes --- prayer time accuracy,
adhan reliability, advertising --- that recur throughout our own results. Both
analyse one application (279 and 2,459 reviews); ours analyses 26 applications
and 732,194 reviews with aspect-based sentiment, seeded topic modelling,
demand-signal extraction, and mixed-effects models rather than content-analysis
themes alone, which is what lets us distinguish an ecosystem-level finding from
an artefact of one application's release history (\S\ref{sec:rq1-coding}). We
position our contribution as filling that gap, not as the first study of
Islamic mobile applications in HCI.

\subsection{Gamification and Self-Tracking}

A substantial literature documents that gamified self-tracking --- streaks,
points, badges --- can produce exactly the harm RQ1 tests for.
\citet{renfree2016dontkick} show that streaks and reminders sustain new
behaviour but create a dependency whose loss introduces fragility into a user's
change effort, and \citet{weathers2023activitystreaks} characterise what makes
a streak psychologically sticky, including framing it as a daily rather than
cumulative task. In interview studies of wearable-tracker users, both
\citet{humphreys2026toxic} and \citet{demetrovics2025lifestylepressures}
report internal conflict organised around anxiety, guilt, loss of bodily
autonomy, and what the latter names ``behavioral conformity and goal
obsession.'' Experimentally, \citet{bai2024darkside} find competition-based
gamification in fitness apps increases stress relative to a
self-exploration-oriented design. Against this, a meta-analysis by
\citet{six2021gamification} finds no significant difference in
depressive-symptom outcomes between gamified and non-gamified mental-health
apps, so the harm is not universal even within self-tracking.

We engage this literature on its strongest terms: the mechanisms it identifies
--- dependency on an artificial streak, anxiety at a data mismatch with lived
experience, conformity to an externally imposed goal --- are well-evidenced in
fitness and habit-tracking contexts. RQ1 asks whether the same mechanism
operates where the tracked behaviour is a religious obligation rather than a
personal fitness goal, and finds that it largely does not (\S\ref{sec:rq1}). We
read this as a boundary condition rather than a refutation: the harm this
literature documents may depend on the tracked behaviour being one the user set
for themselves, which does not describe a prayer obligation.

\subsection{App-Store Review Mining as Method}

Mining app-store reviews is an established method with a maturing literature.
\citet{dabrowski2022analysing} survey 182 papers published between 2012 and
2020, classifying the field by information mined, techniques applied, and
activity supported; \citet{alsubaihin2019appstore} study how the store reshapes
developers' release and requirements practices;
\citet{ciurumelea2017analyzing} build a review taxonomy validated across 39
applications; and \citet{memon2023appstoremining} combine topic modelling,
sentiment analysis, and functional/non-functional classification in a pipeline
structurally similar to ours, on a general consumer-app sample.

The closest precedent for our scale and aspect-level design is
\citet{haggag2022mhealth}, who extract over five million reviews for 278
mHealth applications, classify them into 14 aspect categories, and validate by
hand-analysing 1,000 reviews per aspect. We follow their basic move ---
ecosystem-scale mining resolved to named aspects rather than global sentiment
--- in a domain their sample does not cover, and depart on one point that
matters for RQ2: several of their headline findings are stated as shares of
users awarding one star to an aspect, treating the star rating as a readable
index of severity. Our corpus does not support that assumption
(\S\ref{sec:rq2}), though the effect may be specific to defects users are
unwilling to let tank an app they otherwise value. Their gold samples are also
far larger than ours, which is why we report per-aspect precision with
intervals (\S\ref{sec:method-measurement}) rather than a single figure.

\citet{guluzade2024eating} pair a functionality audit of 27 mindfulness-eating
and eating-disorder applications with a content analysis of 1,248 reviews,
where the tracked behaviour is personally sensitive. Their two-source design, a
hand-built functionality matrix read against what reviewers say, is structurally
our promise-versus-delivery comparison (\S\ref{sec:method-promise}), and their
concern with tracking that misrepresents a user's situation is the nearest
analogue we have found to RQ4's menstrual-exemption gap outside a religious
setting. Our contribution relative to theirs is scale and inference, letting us
report how weakly the two promise sources agree rather than treat a
functionality matrix as ground truth.

Beyond the devotional domain, this paper adds two risks inherent to the method
that the literature has not, to our knowledge, quantified directly: star
ratings systematically hide a class of substantive complaint
(\S\ref{sec:rq2}), and single-app qualitative sampling can manufacture themes
reading as genre-level findings (\S\ref{sec:rq1-coding}).

\subsection{Feature Bloat and Interface Complexity}

That more features degrade user experience is a widely held assumption in
software and product design, more often asserted than tested.
\citet{marzi2022overfeaturing} reviews the ``over-featuring'' literature
across the terms it goes by --- feature creep, feature fatigue, overdesign,
scope creep, gold-plating --- and finds the phenomenon ranked among the top ten
risks of new-product-development failure alongside ``a lack of theoretical
development, and a limited investigation of [its] causes, drivers, and
performance effects.'' In one of the few empirical investigations,
\citet{blackler2016rtfm} find over-featured consumer products do produce
negative emotional experiences and that most users do not exercise most
features. RQ5 tests the commonly assumed causal link --- that a higher feature
count predicts more bloat complaints --- against corpus data, and finds it
fails in both directions (\S\ref{sec:rq5}). Read alongside Marzi's observation
that the phenomenon is asserted more often than investigated, our result
suggests the assumption has outrun its evidence, at least in this domain.
