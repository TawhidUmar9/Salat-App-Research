\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Salah, the ritual prayer performed five times daily, is obligatory for
practising Muslims. Each of the five prayer windows moves with the sun and the
observer's location, and a few further windows depend on more computation ---
times when prayer is disallowed, or when a travelling or menstruating person is
exempt. A large and growing population uses mobile apps to handle this:
computing prayer times, sounding the call to prayer (adhan), pointing toward
Mecca (qibla), and increasingly logging whether a prayer was performed. That
makes these apps' failures different in kind from a productivity app's. A
missed prompt is not a missed reminder; for a user relying on it, it can be a
missed prayer.

The small existing literature on these apps, and the larger literature on the
design patterns they borrow, offers three ways of thinking about what makes
them succeed or fail. The self-tracking and gamification literature says
streaks and completion scores --- mechanics a prayer tracker borrows from
fitness apps --- create guilt and anxiety in users who cannot keep them up.
Product design treats feature richness and interface minimalism as one
trade-off, with a richer feature set winning and keeping users. And accuracy is
often treated as competing with aesthetics, as if tolerance for a wrong prayer
time and tolerance for a cluttered screen were the same preference. Each
framing is plausible and has real evidence behind it in adjacent domains, and
each turns out to mislocate where satisfaction with a devotional app actually
comes from.

What is missing is evidence at ecosystem scale. The closest existing work is
small: a dissertation study of prayer-app design \citep{bellar2017ipray}, a
two-app heuristic comparison \citep{azahari2025comparative}, and two
single-application content analyses of review themes
\citep{noor2025halaltourism,noor2026halalsuperapps}. None mines reviews across
the ecosystem with aspect-level sentiment, and none tests the
gamification-harm hypothesis in a devotional context.

We analyse 732,194 reviews across 26 Islamic prayer-time and devotional apps on
the Google Play Store, addressing six pre-specified research questions:
aspect-based sentiment across 27 aspects, topic modelling, demand-signal
extraction, promise-versus-delivery comparison against store descriptions, and
five statistical models under a shared false-discovery-rate correction,
corroborated by two independently drawn qualitative coding passes.

Three of the six research questions overturn a framing from the literature.
Gamifying a prayer tracker neither helps nor harms sentiment, and guilt
language is \emph{rarer}, not commoner, inside tracker discourse; what users
resent is a record they cannot trust and a prompt that never arrives (RQ1,
\S\ref{sec:rq1}). Accuracy and interface complaints cost the same \emph{as
classes}, but that average hides a severity gradient, and a large share of
accuracy complaints arrive inside four- and five-star reviews, invisible to a
rating-based read of severity (RQ2, \S\ref{sec:rq2}). And feature count does
not predict interface-bloat complaints in either direction; bloat is a property
of the interface, shown directly by bloat and interface complaints co-occurring
far above base rate within individual reviews (RQ5, \S\ref{sec:rq5}). Two
further questions test whether distinctive features confer competitive
advantage (they do not; users cite core reliability instead, RQ3,
\S\ref{sec:rq3}) and whether bio-spiritual needs such as menstrual
accommodation are met (they are not, though with only three implementing
applications we treat this as an unmet-need finding rather than a causal one,
RQ4, \S\ref{sec:rq4}). A sixth question synthesises these into two distinct
failures --- a coverage gap and a delivery gap that need different remedies
(RQ6, \S\ref{sec:rq6}).

This paper makes four contributions: an ecosystem-scale account of
devotional-app quality across six pre-specified research questions, reporting
every non-inferable term rather than dropping it; a reframing of gamification
harm in religious practice as record integrity and prompting reliability rather
than guilt; a demonstration that devotional register breaks off-the-shelf
sentiment and aspect-extraction tooling; and two sampling hazards in app-store
research, each demonstrated with numbers rather than asserted --- star ratings
are an unsafe proxy for defect severity, and single-application sampling can
manufacture what look like genre-level themes.
