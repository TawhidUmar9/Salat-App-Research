\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Salah, the ritual prayer performed five times daily, is obligatory for
practising Muslims and timed astronomically: each of the five windows moves
with the sun and the observer's location, and further windows --- during which
prayer is disallowed, or a travelling or menstruating person is exempt ---
depend on additional computation and on facts about the user's body and
circumstances. A large and growing population uses mobile applications to
mediate this obligation, computing prayer times, sounding the call to prayer
(adhan), orienting toward Mecca (qibla), and increasingly logging whether a
prayer was performed. That makes these applications' failures different in kind
from a productivity app's. A missed prompt is not a missed reminder; for a user
relying on it, it can be a missed prayer.

The small existing literature on these applications, and the larger literature
on the design patterns they borrow, offers three framings for what makes them
succeed or fail. The self-tracking and gamification literature suggests that
streaks and completion scores --- mechanics a prayer tracker borrows from
fitness apps --- induce guilt and anxiety in users who cannot keep them up.
Product design treats feature richness and interface minimalism as a trade-off
along a single axis, and a richer feature set as a differentiator that wins and
retains users. And accuracy is often opposed to aesthetics, as though tolerance
for a wrong prayer time and tolerance for a cluttered screen were governed by
the same preference. Each framing is plausible, each has real evidence behind
it in adjacent domains, and each mis-locates where satisfaction with a
devotional app comes from.

What is missing is evidence at the scale needed to test these framings against
the whole ecosystem rather than one application. The closest existing work is
small in scope or method: a dissertation study of prayer-app design
\citep{bellar2017ipray}, an expert heuristic comparison of two named Islamic
applications \citep{azahari2025comparative}, and single-application content
analyses of review themes \citep{noor2025halaltourism,noor2026halalsuperapps}.
None mines reviews across the ecosystem with aspect-level sentiment, and none
tests the gamification-harm hypothesis in a devotional context. We claim to be
the first, to our knowledge, to bring ecosystem-scale review mining to these
design questions in this domain, not the first study of Islamic mobile
applications, of app-store reviews, or of gamified self-tracking.

We analyse 732,194 reviews across 26 Islamic prayer-time and devotional
applications on the Google Play Store, addressing six pre-specified research
questions with a mixed-methods pipeline: aspect-based sentiment analysis
across 27 functional and experiential aspects, seeded topic modelling,
regex-based demand-signal extraction, promise-versus-delivery comparison
against store descriptions, and five statistical models under a shared
false-discovery-rate correction, corroborated by two independently drawn
qualitative coding passes over tracker-related complaints.

Three of our six research questions retire a framing and replace it with
something the data supports better. Gamifying a prayer tracker neither helps
nor harms sentiment, and guilt language is \emph{rarer}, not commoner, inside
tracker discourse; what users resent is a record they cannot trust and a prompt
that never arrives (RQ1, \S\ref{sec:rq1}). Accuracy and interface complaints
cost the same \emph{as classes}, but that average conceals a severity gradient,
and a large share of accuracy complaints arrive inside four- and five-star
reviews, invisible to a rating-based read of severity (RQ2, \S\ref{sec:rq2}).
And feature count does not predict interface-bloat complaints in either
direction; bloat is a property of the interface, shown by bloat and interface
complaints co-occurring far above base rate within individual reviews (RQ5,
\S\ref{sec:rq5}). Two further questions test whether distinctive features
confer competitive advantage (they do not; users cite core reliability instead,
RQ3, \S\ref{sec:rq3}) and whether bio-spiritual needs such as menstrual
accommodation are met (they are not, though with three implementing
applications we treat this as an unmet-need rather than a causal finding, RQ4,
\S\ref{sec:rq4}). A sixth synthesises these into a coverage gap and a delivery
gap requiring different remedies (RQ6, \S\ref{sec:rq6}).

This paper makes four contributions: an ecosystem-scale account of
devotional-app quality across six pre-specified research questions, with the
false-discovery-rate denominator and every non-inferable term reported rather
than dropped; a reframing of gamification harm in religious practice as record
integrity, prompting reliability, and the application's model of worship rather
than guilt; a demonstration that devotional register breaks off-the-shelf
sentiment and aspect-extraction tooling, a finding about applying
general-purpose NLP to religious text rather than a data-cleaning footnote; and
two sampling hazards in app-store research, each demonstrated with numbers ---
star ratings are an unsafe proxy for defect severity, and single-application
qualitative sampling can manufacture what look like genre-level themes.
