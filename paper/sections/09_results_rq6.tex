\section{RQ6: Completeness and the Two Gaps}
\label{sec:rq6}

The final research question asks directly whether current apps are ``enough.''
The answer is no, but that single word hides two different failures that call
for different remedies. A \emph{coverage gap} is a market failure: whole
categories of need are served by only a minority of apps despite measurable
demand. A \emph{delivery gap} is a quality failure, and the more damning of the
two, because it falls on promises apps have already made.

\subsection{The Delivery Gap}

We compared each app's store-description promises against the sentiment its
reviews record for that feature. Of 272 promised (application, feature)
pairs, 136 carry enough mentions to judge reliably (\(n \geq 30\)); of those,
18 are broken promises across 11 applications --- a feature the app claims to
offer that draws over 40\% negative sentiment. Table~\ref{tab:rq6-broken} ranks
the break rate by share of promising applications, and
Figure~\ref{fig:rq6a-delivery-gap} shows the full delivery gap. The
eighteenth is \texttt{tracker\_score}, broken in the one application that
promises it; a 1-of-1 ratio carries no weight, so we leave it out of the
table. The two largest broken promises by mention volume are both from Muslim
Pro: its reminders/adhan feature (9,989 mentions, 45.6\% negative) and its
monetisation terms (9,182 mentions, 54.8\% negative).

The two features broken in the most applications, five each, are also the two
most basic: adhan reminders are the core function of a prayer-times app, not an
advanced capability, and monetisation is simply the terms on which the app is
offered. Failure here concentrates in the promise the app is built around, not
in peripheral functionality.

\subsection{The Coverage Gap}

We ranked aspects by an unmet-need score, demand-signal volume multiplied by
the number of applications lacking the feature (Table~\ref{tab:rq6-unmet},
Figure~\ref{fig:rq6b-unmet-needs}). Companion hardware tops the ranking,
followed by menstrual handling, mosque finder, forbidden-times awareness, and
\emph{nafl} (supererogatory) prayer-time support. Four aspects fall below 25\%
ecosystem coverage altogether: calendar sync, companion hardware, qasr travel,
and menstrual handling.

Five aspects clear both bars for adequate service, present in over half the
ecosystem and averaging under 30\% negative sentiment: madhab, monetisation,
qibla, table format, and widgets. Monetisation appears on both this list and
the broken-promises list --- most applications handle payment acceptably,
while a minority handle it badly enough to poison the feature ecosystem-wide.

\subsection{The Same Gaps, Reached Independently}

Each gap is also reached independently by an earlier research question, which
we read as corroboration rather than repetition. Companion hardware tops the
unmet-need ranking, and RQ5 finds hardware sentiment is actually
\emph{better} where it is shipped (\S\ref{sec:rq5}) --- consistent with demand
rather than dissatisfaction with an existing product. Menstrual handling ranks
second and is RQ4's central finding in its own right (\S\ref{sec:rq4}).
Reminders/adhan, tied with monetisation as the most-broken promise, is
independently the dominant tracker-dissatisfaction theme in RQ1's cross-app
coding, at 46\% (\S\ref{sec:rq1}).

\subsection{Verdict}

Current applications are not enough, and they fall short in two different
ways. The coverage gap: entire categories of need --- companion hardware,
menstrual accommodation, travel concession, calendar integration --- are
served by under a quarter of the ecosystem despite measurable, explicitly
voiced demand. The delivery gap: the two most-broken promises are adhan
reminders and monetisation terms, the two things a prayer-times application
most fundamentally offers. Building more features is not indicated. Delivering
the promised ones reliably, and extending coverage to the populations
currently unserved, is.
