\section{RQ6: Completeness and the Two Gaps}
\label{sec:rq6}

The final research question asks directly whether current devotional apps are
enough, in the sense the ``apps under-serve niche needs'' framing implies. The
answer is no, but the single word conceals two structurally different
failures that call for different remedies. A \emph{coverage gap} is a market
failure: entire categories of need are served by a minority of the ecosystem
despite measurable demand. A \emph{delivery gap} is a quality failure, and the
more damning of the two, because it falls on promises apps have already made
--- including, as we show below, the most basic ones.

\subsection{RQ6a: The Delivery Gap}

We compared each application's store-description promises against the
sentiment its reviews record for that feature. Of 272 promised
(application, feature) pairs, 136 carry enough mentions to judge reliably
(\(n \geq 30\)); of those, 18 are broken promises across 11 applications ---
a feature the app claims to offer that draws over 40\% negative sentiment.
Table~\ref{tab:rq6-broken} ranks the break rate by share of promising
applications, and Figure~\ref{fig:rq6a-delivery-gap} shows the full delivery
gap. The two largest broken promises by mention volume are both from Muslim
Pro: its reminders/adhan feature (9,989 mentions, 45.6\% negative) and its
monetisation terms (9,182 mentions, 54.8\% negative).

One further aspect, \texttt{tracker\_score}, shows 1 of 1 promising
applications broken. We report this for completeness but do not present it as
the most-broken feature: with a single promising application, the ratio
carries no statistical weight.

The point worth making explicitly is that the two features broken in the most
applications, five each, are also the two most basic. Adhan reminders are the core function of a
prayer-times application, not an advanced capability; monetisation is simply
the terms on which the application is offered. Failure in this ecosystem
concentrates in the promise the app is built around, not in peripheral or
advanced functionality.

\subsection{RQ6b: The Coverage Gap}

We separately ranked aspects by an unmet-need score --- demand-signal volume
multiplied by the number of applications lacking the feature --- built from
the demand-extraction pipeline validated at 91.5\% precision
(\S\ref{sec:method-demand}), rather than from the noisier raw aspect counts
that several low-precision aspects would otherwise force us to rely on
(Table~\ref{tab:rq6-unmet}, Figure~\ref{fig:rq6b-unmet-needs}). Companion
hardware tops the ranking, followed by menstrual handling, mosque finder,
forbidden-times awareness, and \emph{nafl} (supererogatory) prayer-time
support. Four aspects fall below 25\% ecosystem coverage altogether:
\texttt{calendar\_sync}, \texttt{companion\_hardware}, \texttt{qasr\_travel},
and \texttt{women\_period}.

\subsection{Some Needs Are Served Well}

Five aspects clear both bars we set for adequate service: present in over half
the ecosystem, and averaging under 30\% negative sentiment across the
applications where they are mentioned --- madhab, monetisation, qibla, table
format, and widgets. Table format is tagged at 44\% precision, so its place on
this list is the least secure of the five. The average here is taken over
applications rather than
over reviews, which matters: under review-weighted pooling, monetisation,
widgets, and qibla all exceed 30\%, because a few high-volume applications
handle them badly. We report the app-level figure because the question this
list answers is how many applications serve a need adequately, not how many
reviews are unhappy, but the two readings differ and we do not want the choice
to pass unnoticed.

Monetisation appears on both this list and the broken-promises list in
Table~\ref{tab:rq6-broken}, and we read the tension as informative rather than
contradictory. Across the same 16 applications that promise it and carry
\(n \geq 30\) mentions, the break rate is 5 of 16 (31\%) against a mean negative
rate of 25.8\%: most applications handle payment acceptably while a minority
handle it badly enough to poison the feature ecosystem-wide.

\subsection{Synthesis: The Same Gaps, Reached Independently}

Each of the two gaps identified here is also reached independently by an
earlier research question, which we read as corroboration rather than
repetition. Companion hardware tops the unmet-need ranking, and RQ5
(\S\ref{sec:rq5}) finds that hardware sentiment is in fact \emph{better}
inside the one application that ships a device (37.7\% negative) than across
the 23 that do not (49.2\% negative) --- consistent with this being demand
rather than dissatisfaction with an existing product. Menstrual handling
ranks second on the coverage gap and is RQ4's (\S\ref{sec:rq4}) central
finding in its own right. Reminders/adhan is one of the two most-broken promises here, tied with
monetisation at five broken applications each
and is independently the dominant tracker-dissatisfaction theme in RQ1's
cross-app coding, at 46\% (\S\ref{sec:rq1}).

\subsection{Verdict}

Current applications are not enough, and they fall short in two different
ways that call for different responses. The coverage gap is a market failure:
entire categories of need --- companion hardware, menstrual accommodation,
travel concession, calendar integration --- are served by under a quarter of
the ecosystem despite measurable, explicitly voiced demand. The delivery gap
is a quality failure, and the more damning of the two, because it falls on
promises applications have already made: the features most often broken are
adhan reminders and monetisation, the two things a prayer-times application
most fundamentally offers. Building more features is not indicated.
Delivering the promised ones reliably, and extending coverage to the
populations currently unserved, is.
